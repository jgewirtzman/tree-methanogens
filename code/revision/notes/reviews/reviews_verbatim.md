## Editor (Martin De Kauwe)

> Thank you for submitting your work to New Phytologist. After careful consideration of the reviewers' comments, I have decided to reject the manuscript with resubmission encouraged if you feel you can address their comments.
>
> The reviews raise several constructive points, and while many can be addressed through careful revision, greater attention is needed to the **framing of the manuscript and its linkage to plant sciences**. At present, much of the results and interpretation rely heavily on microbial and biochemical explanations, with comparatively less emphasis on plant physiological or structural controls. For example, **how do differences among plant species drive variation in stem methane flux or associated microbial activity?** Overall, while I agree with the reviewers on the importance of the work, I am not yet convinced that the current framing fully aligns the manuscript with the scope and expectations of the journal.

## Referee 1

This is a very good article, which certainly deserves to be published in the New Phytologist. It is one of the most comprehensive studies of the CH4 balance in tree trunks in temperate forest ecosystems, which supports the analysis of the tree stem microbiome published in Nature by Arnold et al 2025. This makes Yale-Myers Forest one of the best-studied forest ecosystems in the world. However, given the somewhat different results of the CH4 balance in stem fluxes obtained in several other studies (see e.g. Gauci et al Nature 2024), further studies are warranted. A more comprehensive study of the microbiome of cryptogamic communities in tree stems would probably be helpful (see e.g. Jeffrey's works on Melaleuca tree trunks).

Also interesting is the higher CH4 flux of the birch species *Betula alleghaniensis* and *B. papyrifera*, which prefer organic soils, which coincides with the higher CH4 flux of other birch species growing on swampy soils (see, for example, Ranniku et al 2023 Environmental Research Communications; https://doi.org/10.1088/2515-7620/acd7c7)

It is advisable to add a corresponding discussion to the discussion section of the article.

## Referee 2

The manuscript investigated the relationships of methane fluxes, methane-cycling microbial communities, and meteorological conditions of upland forest trees. To do so, the authors studied 16 tree species across hydrological gradients in northeastern USA through stem and soil methane flux measurement, stem gas measurement, quantification of methanogens and methanotrophs, and 16S rRNA gene-based community profiling.

The main findings of the study are 1) methanogens and methanotrophs are widely present in wood; 2) methanogens are enriched in wood relative to soils; 3) internal methanogenesis likely contributes substantially to methane emission from upland tree stems inferred by height-dependent flux patterns across hydrological conditions; and 4) the ratio methanogen:methanotroph moderately predicts emission.

Overall, the study is excellently conducted and represents an advance to better understand the mechanism underlying methane fluxes from trees. The large-scale characterization of the wood associated microbial communities served an important bridge of geochemical measurements and biological processes.

However, I have some major comments before recommending acceptance, particularly on the analysis and interpretation of microbial community data.

### 1. Quantification of methanogens and methanotrophs

For most of the studied samples, mcrA, pmoA, and mmoX genes were present at a very low levels, often in the scale of 10 - 100 gene copies per gram of samples. These are very low numbers to be confidently quantified. These "low-biomass" samples are also prone to contamination. What is the confidence of detection of methods used? Did negative controls produce any quantifiable numbers? **Negative control data should be reported explicitly.**

The unit also needs to be clarified, i.e. per gram dry weight or wet weight. It appears dry weight is used for wood but wet weight for soil. To reduce confusion, please to use the same unit.

**pmoA and mmoX abundance should be reported separately** as many methanotrophs encode both pmoA and mmoX. Methanotrophs exclusively encoding mmoX are generally rare (typically limited to only some *Methylocella* and *Methyloferula*).

Gene name such as *mcrA* and *pmoA* should be italicized throughout.

**L248-249:** Please report the primer pairs sequences of pmoA and mmoX within the methods without the readers having to jump between various literatures

### 2. Classification and interpretation of methanotrophs based on 16S rRNA gene

The classification of methanotrophs based on 16S rRNA gene in the study is debatable, especially Beijerinckiaceae and Methylacidiphilaceae that consist of many non-methanotrophic members. How were putative Beijerinckiaceae methanotrophs be classified from genus data? It seems appropriate to **provide a supplementary table for the taxa that were classified as known and putative methanotrophs and methanogens.**

For Methylacidiphilaceae, only geothermal Methylacidiphilaceae has been known to be methanotrophic. All reported mesophilic Methylacidiphilaceae genomes reported to-date do not encode genes for methanotrophy, e.g. peatland (https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9122566) and tree stem (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adu2182).

### 3. Caveats of 16S rRNA gene based functional prediction

The caveats of 16S rRNA gene based functional prediction should be made explicit. Many, if not most, functions are very poorly predicted by only community composition (especially when 16S data couldn't even resolve genus and species resolution for many taxa), including fermentation and hydrogen oxidation. It is very unlikely that only a minor fraction of the wood community could perform fermentation in a hypoxic habitat. "Dark" hydrogen oxidation is predicted to be only 3.6% in heartwood, yet hydrogenotrophic methanogens that oxidizes hydrogen for methane production on average already on par with this number.

### 4. Potential misunderstanding of some microbiology concepts

**L412-413:** Many Beijerinckiaceae genera especially *Methylocapsa* do not encode soluble MMO and cannot grow on multi-carbon substrates.

**L623:** Both reductive acetyl-CoA pathway and RuMP cycle are not directly associated with fermentation. The former is an anaerobic carbon-fixation pathway and the latter is associated with one-carbon compound assimilation.

**L695-700:** Perhaps it's also worth keeping in mind that pMMO and sMMO are distinguished by their affinity towards CH4. sMMO is low-affinity enzyme, which the threshold of CH4 oxidation is usually > 500 ppm. Methanotrophs that use sMMO likely only able to oxidize CH4 that is present at a significantly enriched level.

### 5. Open availability of data

The manuscript reports a wealth of data from methane fluxes, internal gas measurements to tree and site characterization. However, none of the raw data were made available. The authors are highly encouraged to make these data available for appraisal, but also to facilitate reanalysis in future research effort.

### 6. Figure issues

**Fig. 2A** X-axis scale is non-intuitive and inconsistent as the scale is neither linear or typical log scale. For example, it appears to linear in the case of *Quercus rubra*, but not in *Carya ovata*. The authors should ensure consistency in x-axis scale. Flux distributions for some tree species also significantly distorted by outliers, such as *Acer rubrum* and *Quercus rubra*. The dotted line in panels should be explicitly defined.

**Fig. 4** Both panel a and c are missing appropriate legends for colors in stacked bar charts.

**Fig. 7b** Why was mcrA copies per uL used instead of mcrA copies per gram? The former is variable depending on both elution volume and sample quantity used for DNA extraction.

**Fig. 7c** Missing unit for CH4 flux

### 7. Other comments

The current **title is too non-specific and almost reads like a review article**. The authors are recommended to provide a more descriptive title that reflects their findings.

**L607-610:** The interpretation of 13C-CH4 isotopic data requires more caution. Methanogenesis can produce a wide range of δ13C-CH4 value and the 13C depletion is controlled also by the δ13C of the carbon source (e.g. CO2) which was not measured in this study. Appropriate references should also be cited to support the measured δ13C-CH4 is more in line with hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis.

## Referee 3

Gewirtzman et al have prepared a substantial study of tree stem microbiomes, focused on methano-bugs in sapwood and heartwood, paired with stem methane flux data. Their stated goals are (cut and pasted from text):
- quantify tree methane emissions across environmental gradients, using vertical profiles to differentiate soil-derived transport from internal production
- characterize methanogenic and methanotrophic communities across tree species and tissue
- establish microbial gene abundance-flux relationships at individual and species levels
- evaluate ecosystem-scale impacts by upscaling measurements to estimate forest-wide fluxes

A great deal of effort went into data collection on tree stem methane biogeochemistry and microbiology in the Yale-Myers Forest. This is likely a globally unique dataset in terms of the volume of methane cycling genes assessed at a single forest site. Paired with the stem methane flux makes this is a strong contribution to the field's emerging understanding of the role woody surfaces play in land-atmosphere methane exchange.

There are several weaknesses of the study in its current form. The results and discussion **lean rather microbial heavy and plant light.** For example, the word 'species' is used, referring to tree species, 7 times in the introduction and study objectives without any reference to what it is about different species that might affect stem methane flux.

Because there are multiple years of data collected with distinct strategies and objectives, it is hard to track what data and results go with which efforts and levels of replication. For instance, **there are data presented from a felled tree, but no mention of this in the methods.** The organization of the paper (methods sections in the supplemental material, number of figures, and overall length) mute the potential of the data.

Lastly, the use of rather **weak random forest model to make an upscaled forest-wide flux estimate seems unhelpful to me.** This aligns with a tendency towards overstating claims and the absolute magnitude of potential stem methane flux in global methane cycling throughout the text. To be clear, this is an important and interesting process to understand, but cautious and appropriate interpretation of the data is needed.

From the data presented, the goals of the study are overly ambitious. **Reduction in stem methane flux with height does not necessarily imply a soil-source of the gas**, though it does seem plausible. A trend with height for *Betula alleghaniensis* is used as evidence that wetter soil, and thus methane of soil-origin, dominates over internal production in this species. But this glosses over other tree species found in wetter soils that do not show this trend. Additionally, *B. alleghaniensis* has the 4th highest mcrA gene abundance in its heartwood. Are the highest *B. alleghaniensis* fluxes found in the wettest soil? It doesn't appear so from Figure 3. What would make this study particularly impactful would be to assess what aspects of *B. alleghaniensis* wood chemistry or morphology support a reduction in stem flux with height. Could this be an effect of rooting depth? After reading the study, I am confident that methane is both produced and consumed within stems (though this was already known), but what properties control the emissions from the woody surface remain unknown. This is underscored by their own data, which generates a random forest model explaining 28% of soil flux variability from 276 measurements, and 15% of stem flux variability from 1,148 measurements.
